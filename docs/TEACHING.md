# Teaching design

## Learner model

The primary learner is highly curious, approximately 14–17 years old, and
comfortable with algebra but new to quantum mechanics. The project assumes
intellectual ambition, not prior vocabulary.

The app should never reward speed, memorized terminology, or passive scrolling.
Its currency is discovery: noticing an invariant, falsifying a prediction, or
finding which assumption made something impossible.

## Interaction cycle

Every major lab should follow:

1. **Predict** — commit to an outcome.
2. **Run** — manipulate one meaningful variable.
3. **Notice** — expose a visible or numerical consequence.
4. **Explain** — connect the consequence to a state, equation, or invariant.
5. **Break it** — perturb the construction and observe which rule fails.

## Three explanation depths

- **Intuition:** a visual claim that can be tested.
- **Mechanism:** the equation or algorithm producing the effect.
- **Prove it:** an exact cancellation, invariant, or counterexample.

These layers should be available in that order without implying that the visual
metaphor replaces the mathematics.

## Scientific language rules

Prefer:

- “a complex vector whose projections determine outcome probabilities”;
- “relative phase becomes observable after recombination”;
- “entanglement means the joint pure state does not factor”;
- “maximal entanglement across every two-versus-two cut”;
- “a quantum generalization of the classical problem.”

Avoid:

- “a particle is literally in two classical states” as an established fact;
- “measurement requires consciousness”;
- “entanglement sends an instant message”;
- “every pair is entangled with every other pair” for AME(4,6);
- “balanced colors and shapes are the complete QOLS conditions”;
- “quantum mechanics solves Euler’s original classical parade.”

## Review rubric for a new lab

Ask:

- What exact learner question does it answer?
- What can the learner change?
- What visible quantity responds?
- What mathematical object is being modeled?
- Which claim is theorem, operational fact, simulation assumption, or
  interpretation?
- Can the learner break the construction and diagnose why?
- Does it work by keyboard and without color perception?
- Can its central claim be tested deterministically?

## Educator use

The existing sequence works as a 45–75 minute guided exploration or as
individual stations:

- 10 minutes: classical constraints and impossibility;
- 15 minutes: amplitude, Born probabilities, and phase;
- 15 minutes: tensor products and entanglement;
- 15–30 minutes: Golden QOLS, coherence failure, and perfect tensors.

Encourage learners to write a prediction before pressing a simulation button.
The most productive debrief question is not “Did you get it right?” but “Which
assumption made your prediction fail?”
